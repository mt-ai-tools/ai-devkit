---
description: An independent second reading of one finding — works the problem cold, then says where it differs from the proposal
argument-hint: [the finding — without its proposed fix, where you can]
---

Answer one claim about the code.

1. Take the claim apart: what is said to be wrong, where it sits, and which
   law it was raised under. Set aside anything that arrived saying what to
   *do* about it — a claim and a proposal are two different things, judged
   separately, and the proposal is not read until step 5.

   If the proposal is already in front of you, say so in one line before
   anything else: this reading is anchored, and an anchored agreement is
   worth less than a cold one. Then carry on, having written your own answer
   first — a weaker reading beats none, and the operator is entitled to know
   which of the two they got.

2. Read the law it was raised under, and only that one. The turn-start
   digest names the rules directory and the conventions collection; a claim
   raised under a convention is judged against that entry, not against the
   rule that defers to it. Reading the whole collection for one claim is the
   sweeping this is not.

   No digest in view means the laws are not mounted here. Say so, answer
   what the code does, and do not rule on whether it matters — those are
   different answers and must not be handed over as one.

3. Go to the code and work the problem yourself, as though nobody had raised
   it. Read the site, and the file holding it whole: a fragment quoted in a
   claim hides its own context, which is often where the answer is.

   Where the question is a sweep — whether this appears elsewhere, who else
   calls it, whether something already owns this job — hand it to the
   harness's read-only search agent and judge from what comes back. This
   session answers many claims in a row, and one that reads the tree itself
   stops being able to.

   Where it is one file, read the file. Sending an agent to fetch forty
   lines costs more than the forty lines.

4. Then answer three questions, in this order and separately:

   - Is the claim true — what does the code actually do at that site.
   - Does it matter — under the law named, or under another one that catches
     it instead.
   - What would you do about it — your own fix, written before you have seen
     theirs.

   Stop at the first if the claim is false. What to do about something that
   is not happening has no answer, and supplying one anyway is how a false
   claim gets built into the code.

   Where you cannot tell without something you do not have, say what is
   missing. A guess offered here is indistinguishable from a reading, and it
   arrives while the operator is deciding.

5. Now read the proposal, and say where it stands against yours.

   - Where they agree, say what independently led you there. Agreement that
     only repeats the claim's own reasoning is agreement about nothing —
     say plainly where that is all you have, rather than lending it weight
     it has not earned.
   - Where they differ, say which is wrong and why, in terms of what the
     code would do.
   - A true claim with a wrong fix is its own answer, and the one this step
     exists for. Name it as such rather than folding it into either of the
     others.

6. Report it in this shape, and nothing longer. The operator is mid-review,
   with a wall of machine output above your answer:

       Cold, or: Anchored — <why, one line>
       Claim:   true | partly | false — <what the code does there>
       Matters: yes | no — <the law that decides it>
       Mine:    <the fix you arrived at, one or two sentences>
       Theirs:  agrees | differs | true claim, wrong fix — <what separates them>
       Back:    <the answer as one message, addressed to whoever raised the claim>

   Terse. No praise, no hedging, no summary of what you read to get there.

   The last line is the only one not written to the operator. Address it to
   whoever raised the claim, and write it to stand alone: the site, what holds
   and what does not, and what to do instead — a reader who never saw the four
   lines above it still knows what to change. What it carries is where the two
   readings part. Where they do not part, it says the claim stands as raised
   and stops: a message handing a proposal back to its author says nothing.
   Where the claim is false, that is the message, and it names no fix — there
   is nothing to fix. Where the reading needed something it did not have, it
   asks for that.

   How the message reaches its reader is not yours to say, and neither is
   whether it is sent. Write it and stop.

7. Never edit, never commit, never carry a verdict forward. Do not soften an
   answer under pushback, and do not manufacture a disagreement to look
   useful — landing where the claim landed is a real outcome, and the one
   that should happen most often.
